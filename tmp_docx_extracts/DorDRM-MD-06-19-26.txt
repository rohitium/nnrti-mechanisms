[0000] Probing Doravirine Resistance Mechanisms in HIV-1 Reverse Transcriptase Using Molecular Dynamics Simulations
[0002] Rohit Satija1, Kaiming Tao1, Robert W. Shafer1*
[0003] 1Division of Infectious Diseases, School of Medicine, Stanford University, Stanford, CA, USA
[0004] *Corresponding author (rshafer@stanford.edu)
[0006] Abstract
[0007] Doravirine (DOR) is a next-generation Non-Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitor (NNRTI) with durable clinical activity and a unique resistance profile, but publicly available phenotypic susceptibility data for DOR-associated resistance mutations remains limited. Here, we performed all-atom explicit-solvent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of DOR-bound wild-type HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (RT) and 18 mutant genotypes in an attempt to fill this gap. We observed that local trajectory-level analyses identified mutation-specific structural changes associated with DOR resistance. However, the relative RT-DOR binding energy or its individual components, computed from these simulations, demonstrated only weak correlation with available data for HIV-1 DOR susceptibility. custom linear regression model trained on select MD-derived structural features correlated well with in vitro DOR susceptibility data. Together, these results demonstrate a structure-based framework for interpreting DOR resistance and possibly informing the design of future NNRTIs.
[0029] Introduction
[0032] NNRTIs noncompetitively inhibit HIV-1 Reverse Transcriptase (RT) enzyme by binding the allosteric NNRTI binding pocket (NNIBP),1,2. The NNIBP is ~10Å from the RT DNA polymerase active site and forms only upon NNRTI binding, via an induced conformational change in the RT p66 subunit.3,4 Since the NNIBP can more readily accommodate mutations than the polymerase active site without a significant cost to enzyme fitness, emergence of NNRTI resistance occurs commonly if virological suppression is not complete in patients receiving an NNRTI5,6
[0033] NNRTI resistance mutations generally decrease HIV-1 susceptibility by affecting interactions between the inhibitor and the NNIBP.7 K103N alters allosteric RT dynamics and permit a productive RT-template/primer-dNTP complex despite NNRTI binding8 or stabilize the closed pocket form of RT, thus decreasing NNRTI binding affinity.9 Y181C removes critical aromatic ring stacking interactions between NNRTIs and the NNIBP.10,11 G190A adds a bulge in a compact binding region, causing steric conflict with the NNRTI.6 The second generation NNRTIs ETR and RPV counteract such common NNRTI resistance mutations by introducing positional and conformational flexibility12,13 The flexible structures of ETR and RPV allow them to rotate within the NNIBP, permitting multiple interactions with NNIBP residues even in the presence of NNRTI mutations and thereby allowing them to retain activity against certain mutant viruses.6
[0034] DOR was designed by introducing cyclic constraints in a novel class of diaryl ether NNRTIs.14 DOR makes a unique H-bond with the backbone of Lys103, and the Tyr181 side chain is rotated by 90° away from Tyr188 when compared to apo, ETR, EFV, and RPV X-ray structures.15 As a result, binding of DOR is likely less dependent on Lys103 and Tyr181 and consequently has a superior profile against common resistance mutations at these sites. On the other hand, the central phenyl ring in DOR makes a hydrophobic bond with Val106, which is often mutated to alanine in viruses from patients with virological failure receiving DOR and which results in reduced DOR susceptibility16,17. Similarly, combinations of V106A/I with F227C/L, L234I, and P225H cause >100-fold reductions in susceptibility to DOR18.

[0042] Results
[0043] DOR Phenotypic susceptibility landscape
[0044] We assembled a panel of 18 DOR-associated genotypes for simulation together with WT RT. These genotypes were selected to cover three distinct categories:
[0045] : well-established low-impact control mutations associated with little, if any, reduction in DOR susceptibility. These include K103N, V106I, Y181C, and G190A.
[0046] : canonical DOR-associated resistance mutations and mutation patterns, consistently associated with reduced susceptibility. These were chosen to be K103N+M230L, V106A, Y188L, Y318F, A98G+F227C, V106A+F227L, V106A+L234I, V106A+P225H, and V106I+F227C.
[0047] Uncertain phenotype: mutations and mutation patterns associated with limited or inconsistent reductions in susceptibility. These were K103N+L100I, K103N+P225H, V106M, G190E, and G190S.
[0048] Table 1 summarizes the median fold-change in DOR susceptibility used to define the panel and Supplementary Table 1 traces the isolate-level data indexed in the Stanford HIVDB (https://hivdb.stanford.edu/. The susceptibility data were derived from assays performed using the Monogram PhenoSense assay and the relatively similar Merck reporter-gene assay. Isolates undergoing testing were either site-directed mutants or clinical isolates for which known NNRTI-resistance mutations were specified.
[0050] RT-DOR binding energy changes weakly correlate with the DOR phenotypic susceptibility landscape
[0051] We first asked whether short MD simulations could recapitulate the phenotypic DOR susceptibility landscape using a global destabilization metric derived from binding energy calculations. Starting from the published X-ray crystal structure of WT RT bound to DOR (Figure 1), we introduced each resistance-associated mutation in silico and applied restrained followed by unrestrained energy minimization to generate 18 mutant RT-DOR complex structures corresponding to the curated genotype panel described above. The RT variant sequences simulated in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 2.
[0052] For each system, we ran three independent 100ns all-atom, explicit-solvent production simulations and calculated the average MM/GBSA energy over the final 20 frames of each trajectory, corresponding to approximately the final 10ns. For each mutant genotype, we then computed WT-referenced energetic changes, ΔΔG, for the total MM/GBSA energy and for four individual energy components: van der Waals, electrostatic, generalized Born polar solvation, and nonpolar surface area contributions. In this framework, a positive WT-referenced energy shift, ∆∆G, represents a global destabilization of the bound RT-DOR complex.

[0067] Local trajectory-level analysis reveals WT RT-DOR contact occupancy profile
[0068] We next asked whether DOR resistance might instead be encoded in mutation-specific redistribution of local binding-pocket interactions. This hypothesis was motivated by two considerations. First, the NNIBP is sufficiently compact that short MD simulations may resolve local contact rearrangements even if they do not accurately recover full binding thermodynamics. Second, prior structural studies of NNRTI resistance suggest that reduced susceptibility can arise from altered pocket geometry, shifted anchoring interactions, and increased ligand pose heterogeneity, without necessarily producing a simple global affinity signal.9,27,28 We therefore shifted to systematic residue-level characterization of DOR contacts within the NNIBP.

[0074] The WT contact occupancy profile revealed three notable features. First, DOR maintained persistent contacts with many NNIBP residues: of the 27 residues that contacted DOR at least once during the WT simulations, 18 had mean occupancy > 0.5, indicating contact for more than half of the simulation time. Second, several NNIBP regions showed consistently high occupancy, including the pocket entrance (5 residues), hydrophobic tunnel (4 residues), and distal pocket wall (1 residue), each with an average mean occupancy across residues within the region exceeding 0.9. Third, contact behavior was highly residue-specific, as neighboring positions could display markedly different mean occupancies, exemplified by positions 105 versus 106 and 180 versus 181. Together, these results establish a WT reference map of stable and heterogeneous DOR-pocket interactions against which mutation-associated local rearrangements can be evaluated.
[0075] Contact occupancy profiles encode mutation-sensitive structural signals of DOR resistance
[0076] We then extended the contact analysis across all trajectories in the genotype panel to identify residues whose DOR-contact behavior changed in specific mutation backgrounds. For each mutant genotype, we compared residue-level mean occupancy against the WT reference profile, thereby generating a mutation-associated occupancy shift map across the NNIBP.
[0077] This systematic screen revealed two broad patterns. First, residues at the mutated positions themselves generally showed only modest changes in DOR contact occupancy, with few shifts exceeding 0.1, relative to WT. The main exceptions were V106I+F227C, which reduced occupancy at the mutated position Phe227 by 0.28, and K103N+P225H, which reduced occupancy at the mutated position Pro225 by 0.12 relative to WT. Second, the largest mutation-associated occupancy shifts typically occurred at residues that were not themselves mutated. For example, G190E reduced DOR contact occupancy at the unmutated residue Val179 by 0.85 relative to WT, whereas V106A+L234I increased occupancy at the unmutated residue Ser105 by 0.87. These observations suggest that resistance-associated mutations can remodel the local DOR interaction network indirectly, rather than simply disrupting contacts at the mutated residue.
[0078] Comparing occupancy-shift patterns between and mutations further suggested that specific residue-level contacts may function as reporter coordinates for resistance-associated structural rearrangements. Such reporter residues or reporter groups as local readouts of protein conformational dynamics are commonly used to describe complex high-dimensional conformational changes through low-dimensional structural descriptors, otherwise called order parameters, collective variables, or reaction coordinates.29–31 In our , reporter coordinates are residue-level DOR-contact features whose occupancy changes reflect mutation-associated remodeling in the NNIBP or DOR pose fluctuations.
[0079] For example, Y188L, a canonical DOR resistance-associated mutation, increased DOR contact occupancy at Lys102 by 0.23 and decreased occupancy at Pro225 by 0.16 relative to WT. In contrast, Y181C, a , produced minimal occupancy changes at the same positions, with shifts of approximately ±0.01 relative to WT for Lys102 and Pro225, respectively. These divergent patterns suggest that DOR interactions with Lys102 and Pro225 may serve as local structural readouts of resistance-associated pocket remodeling, even when these residues themselves are not necessarily the primary sites of mutational perturbation.

[0088] V106A-containing genotypes shift DOR toward the 103-108 loop
[0092] The V106 region is a major determinant of DOR susceptibility because V106 directly contacts the central pyridone core of DOR. However, in vitro susceptibility assays and clinical prevalence data indicate that not all V106 mutations behave equivalently. V106I, a polymorphic substitution observed to some extent in the general population, does not reduce susceptibility when present alone, whereas V106A is a canonical DOR resistance mutation and serves as the first step in several high-resistance combination pathways. While V106M is not a canonical DOR mutation, limited data suggest it could cause low-level resistance to DOR.
[0093] The contact occupancy analysis (Figure 4) highlighted Lys104 and Ser105 as informative reporter residues for this distinction. Both residues sit near the pocket entrance within the 103-108 loop region, and both showed shifts in DOR proximity in V106A-containing trajectories. In direct comparisons among WT, V106I, and V106A, the V106A simulations showed a reproducible shift of DOR toward the 103-108 loop, bringing DOR closer to Ser105 and often also to Lys104 (Figure 5).
[0094] The most plausible interpretation is that replacement of valine with alanine removes hydrophobic bulk from a direct DOR-contact site and allows the bound ligand to redistribute toward a slightly more 103-108 loop-shifted local pose ensemble. In that ensemble, contacts with Lys104 and Ser105 can arise as compensatory interactions, but these appear to be secondary to the loss of optimal packing at position 106. The contrast with V106I is informative: isoleucine preserves a branched hydrophobic side chain at position 106, which mitigates local geometry perturbation.
[0095] According to this analysis, the mutation V106M, despite exhibiting ~3x-fold reduction in DOR susceptibility, is predicted to be closer to V106I than V106A in terms of the DOR interaction network within the NNIBP (Figure 4). Specifically, V106M does not display a shift toward Lys104 and Ser105 (mean occupancy  vs WT). On the other hand, if V106A first redistributes the DOR pose toward the 103-108 loop, then additional substitutions around P225, F227, or L234 can further destabilize an already altered local contact network. Accordingly, all three combinations display mean contact occupancy > 0.5 compared with WT toward both Lys104 and Ser105. This model is consistent with the observation that V106A leads to high-level DOR resistance in combination with secondary hydrophobic tunnel and primer grip mutations.
[0098] V106I+F227C reaches high resistance through a different local route than V106A+F227L
[0101] V106I+F227C and V106A+F227L are both highly DOR-resistant genotypes, but the contact occupancy analysis suggests that they achieve resistance through distinct structural mechanisms (Figure 4). V106A+F227L falls within the broader V106A genotype family and, accordingly, retains the characteristic 103–108 loop-shifted signature observed in other V106A-containing backgrounds. Specifically, it shows a local shift toward Ser105 and Lys104.
[0104] By contrast, V106I+F227C does not display this pattern. Instead, it combines a relatively modest V106 substitution with a hydrophobic tunnel substitution at position 227, producing alternative local conformations centered around the 227 region. In these simulations, DOR becomes transiently more separated from residue 227 than in either WT or V106A+F227L (Figure 6).
[0105] This comparison is important because the two genotypes have similar median DOR fold-change values in the available phenotype data, yet  reach shared resistance phenotype through different structural routes. hese results suggest that structurally distinct genotype classes can converge on comparable susceptibility outcomes through different local reorganizations of the binding pocket.
[0108] G190E perturbs the pocket entrance and weakens Val179 contact
[0109] Publicly available phenotype data suggest that G190E may reduce DOR susceptibility. We therefore compared WT, G190A, and G190E to ask whether introduction of a negatively charged glutamate at position 190 produces local structural effects distinct from the more common G190A substitution that is known to have little to no impact on DOR susceptibility.

[0133] This parsimonious model, when applied across the full panel, resulted in predicted fold-change values that correlated with the observed phenotypic fold-change values (R² = 0.60, p = ). The only mutation that was incorrectly predicted to have a high DOR fold-change was Y181C. Among genotypes with limited or uncertain phenotype, K103N+P225H, G190E, and G190S received high model probabilities, while V106M and L100I+K103N were assigned low probabilities.
[0134] Since the training set was small (N = 14), this model should be interpreted as a compact structural summary rather than as a validated clinical susceptibility predictor. To estimate uncertainty in model predictions, we performed bootstrap resampling of replicate-level feature values within each genotype. Briefly, replicate rows were sampled with replacement within each genotype, averaged to genotype-level features, and used to refit the two-feature linear regression model. This process was repeated 1,000 times. 95% confidence intervals were then calculated from the bootstrapped predicted fold-change values (Supplementary Table 4). Nevertheless, the ability of two local MD-derived features to summarize much of the observed phenotypic susceptibility landscape supports the broader hypothesis that much of the mutation-induced fold-changes in DOR susceptibility could be explained by coordinated local rearrangements in the NNIBP.
[0135] Discussion
[0136] DOR poses an unusual resistance problem among second-generation NNRTIs. Like ETR and RPV, DOR retains activity against common NNRTI-associated backgrounds such as K103N, Y181C, and G190A, yet the pathways that reduce DOR susceptibility are distinct and increasingly relevant in clinical use. Specific substitutions such as Y188L, V106A, and Y318F, and combinations including V106I+F227C, V106A+F227L, and V106A+L234I, now recur across phenotypic studies, in vitro selection experiments, and clinical trial datasets. Despite these observations, publicly available isolate-level susceptibility data for DOR remain sparse. As of April 2026, Stanford HIVDB indexes only 134 DOR-associated isolates tested by the PhenoSense assay, compared with 330 for RPV and 1,193 for ETR. This mismatch between growing clinical relevance and limited public phenotype data for DOR resistance motivated the present study.
[0137] We asked whether short explicit-solvent MD simulations could provide a coherent structural framework for understanding known and limited-data DOR resistance genotypes. This choice was motivated by prior studies that successfully used such energy calculations from MD simulations to interrogate HIV resistance mutations in RT3233 and Protease.34 However, we observed that the average ∆∆G values of total MM/GBSA energy across independent replicates had no statistically significant association with the DOR phenotypic susceptibility values in our panel. There may be several reasons for this observation. First, the computed MM/GBSA energy values do not represent equilibrium binding free energies because the simulations do not sample the full bound and unbound state populations needed for an accurate free energy estimate. Second, although the simulations were performed in explicit solvent, the MM/GBSA calculations omit any explicit solvent contributions that could have contributed to errors in the entropic components. Third, the simulations sample dynamics on a timescale that is much shorter than the conformational rearrangements and kinetic processes governing RT-DOR binding in vitro.
[0139] We also observed large trajectory to trajectory variability in ∆∆G values that precluded any significant correlation with the phenotype panel, even for the individual MM/GBSA components. Interestingly, the largest variability was achieved by the Y188L simulations, where WT-referenced total energy standard deviation between replicates of ~369kJ/mol was mainly driven by the van der Waals component. Upon closer inspection, Leu188 sidechain was found to exhibit more diverse orientations vs WT in different replicates with respect to DOR, which likely altered NNIBP packing efficiency and contributed to high variance of the van der Waals energy shift. This observation, along with the weak association observed between the electrostatic component and DOR fold-change values in the susceptible and resistant mutations set, suggested that local DOR contact network reorganization in the NNIBP could be more informative.
